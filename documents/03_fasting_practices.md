# Fasting Practices

## Overview

Fasting is practiced across Indian religious traditions for different reasons. Ekadashi fasts occur twice a month on the eleventh day of the lunar calendar. Navratri involves nine days of restricted eating twice a year. Karva Chauth, Mahashivaratri, and other Hindu observances carry their own fasting protocols. Among India's Muslim population, Ramadan involves daily dawn-to-sunset fasting for one full month. These practices span millennia of observance, and modern metabolic research has begun to identify the physiological mechanisms behind their health effects.

---

## What Happens Metabolically During Fasting

In the first few hours of a fast, the body consumes its glycogen stores through glycogenolysis. After approximately 8 hours, glycogen is depleted and the body enters an adaptive phase. It begins converting glucogenic amino acids into glucose (gluconeogenesis) and converting ketogenic amino acids and fatty acids into ketone bodies (ketogenesis).

This metabolic shift from glucose-dependent to fat-dependent energy metabolism is central to the health effects of fasting. The liver becomes the primary site of this transition, and the products of the shift, including ketone bodies like acetone and acetoacetate, serve as alternative fuel sources for the brain and muscles.

## Evidence from the London Ramadan Study (LORANS)

The London Ramadan Study collected blood samples from 72 participants of diverse ethnic backgrounds (Indian, Pakistani, Somali, Bangladeshi, and Arab) before and after one month of Ramadan fasting. Using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, the researchers measured 169 metabolites across 17 substance classes.

After Ramadan, 14 metabolites showed significant changes. The most important findings were:

**Glycoprotein acetyls decreased significantly.** Glycoprotein acetyls is an inflammation marker influenced by five circulating acute-phase inflammatory glycoproteins. Its reduction is a favorable sign, as elevated glycoprotein acetyls is a predictive marker for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, colorectal cancer, and all-cause mortality.

**Lactate and pyruvate decreased.** These are byproducts of glycolysis. Their reduction confirms the shift away from glucose-based metabolism toward fatty acid and ketone-based metabolism.

**Triglycerides in multiple lipoprotein fractions decreased.** Reductions in triglycerides in HDL, LDL, IDL, and VLDL fractions reflect lipolysis and active breakdown of fatty acids during ketogenesis.

**Acetone increased.** This ketone body increase confirms active ketogenesis, consistent with the liver switching to fatty acid metabolism.

All of these changes were independent of changes in body composition, meaning fasting itself drives the metabolic shift rather than weight loss alone.

---

## Cancer Risk Score Reductions

Using metabolic risk scores built from 117,981 UK Biobank participants, the LORANS researchers found that after Ramadan fasting:

The metabolic risk score for lung cancer decreased by 9.6% (p < 0.001). The score for colorectal cancer decreased by 2.4% (p < 0.001). The score for breast cancer decreased by 1.1% (p = 0.006).

Glycoprotein acetyls was the primary driver of all three reductions. When the inflammation marker was excluded from the models, statistical significance was lost, confirming it as the central mechanism linking fasting to reduced cancer metabolic risk.

## Effects on Body Composition

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 66 studies (Correia et al., Frontiers in Nutrition, 2020) compared Ramadan and non-Ramadan intermittent fasting on body composition:

Ramadan IF produced significant reductions in body weight (SMD = -0.353, p = 0.02) and relative fat mass (SMD = -0.533, p = 0.034) compared to controls.

Non-Ramadan intermittent fasting (time-restricted feeding, alternate-day fasting, and intermittent energy restriction) produced greater reductions in absolute fat mass (SMD = -0.447, p < 0.001) and BMI (SMD = -0.699, p < 0.001).

Non-Ramadan IF also showed a significant increase in fat-free mass when caloric intake was controlled (SMD = 0.351, p < 0.001), suggesting it may be more effective at preserving or building lean tissue compared to Ramadan IF.

The weight loss during Ramadan is attributed to: reduced meal frequency (from 3-4 to 2 meals per day), a metabolic shift toward fatty acid use for ATP synthesis, decreased secretion of anabolic hormones (insulin), and increased secretion of catabolic hormones (adrenaline and glucagon). Importantly, Ramadan IF did not produce significant losses in fat-free mass in the meta-analysis, which is a favorable finding for preserving functional capacity and resting energy expenditure.

## Gut Microbiome and Circadian Effects

Ramadan fasting induces shifts in gut bacterial composition. The gut microbiota plays a central role in metabolic health, immune function, and inflammation regulation. Dietary modifications during the non-fasting hours, combined with the fasting period itself, create a distinct microbial environment that differs from normal eating patterns.

The timing of fasting also aligns with circadian rhythm biology. Eating during daylight hours and fasting at night (as in conventional time-restricted feeding) is associated with improved circadian hormone regulation. Ramadan reverses this to nighttime eating, but the fasting period still creates a sustained phase of reduced insulin signaling and increased fat metabolism.

---

## Gene Expression Changes

Ramadan fasting modulates gene expression in several pathways. Research has shown upregulation of:

**SOD2 and Nrf2:** Antioxidant defense genes that protect cells from oxidative damage.

**SIRT1 and SIRT3:** Sirtuin genes associated with longevity, mitochondrial function, and metabolic regulation.

**TFAM:** Mitochondrial transcription factor associated with mitochondrial biogenesis.

**Autophagy genes:** Dawn-to-dusk fasting was associated with overexpression of autophagy genes, the cellular recycling process that clears damaged proteins and organelles and is associated with reduced cancer and neurodegeneration risk.

---

## Summary of Key Metabolic Benefits

Fasting practices from Indian traditions (Ekadashi, Navratri, Ramadan) share a common physiological mechanism: sustained periods without food force the body to deplete glycogen, shift to fatty acid and ketone metabolism, reduce systemic inflammation, and activate protective gene expression pathways. Modern metabolomics research confirms these short-term metabolic shifts produce measurable reductions in chronic disease risk markers.
