# Turmeric / Curcumin

## What is Turmeric?

Turmeric is the dried rhizome powder of the Curcuma longa plant, a member of the ginger family native to tropical South Asia. It has been used in Indian medicine, cooking, and religious practice for nearly 4000 years. The name derives from the Latin terra merita, referring to the color of ground turmeric, which resembles a mineral pigment. In North India it is called haldi, derived from the Sanskrit haridra.

India produces nearly all of the world's turmeric crop and consumes 80% of it. Erode in Tamil Nadu is the world's largest turmeric trading center, often called Yellow City or Turmeric City.

---

## Chemical Composition

Turmeric contains more than 100 components. The main categories are:

Curcuminoids, which make up the most medically significant fraction. The three primary curcuminoids are curcumin (62-90 mg/g of commercial turmeric), demethoxycurcumin (9-23 mg/g), and bisdemethoxycurcumin (0.3-14 mg/g). Curcumin alone composes approximately 3-8% of the dried root by weight. A standard dessert spoon of turmeric powder (about 3g) contains 30-90 mg of curcumin.

Volatile oils including turmerone, ar-turmerone, and zingiberene, which are responsible for turmeric's aroma and have their own distinct pharmacological activities.

Nutritionally, 100g of turmeric provides 390 kcal, 10g total fat, 69.9g total carbohydrates, 21g dietary fiber, 8g protein, 47.5mg iron, and 50mg ascorbic acid. Turmeric is also a notable source of the omega-3 fatty acid alpha-linolenic acid.

## Curcumin: The Primary Bioactive

Curcumin has the molecular formula C21H20O6 and the chemical name diferuloylmethane. It was first extracted from turmeric in 1815 and its molecular formula was determined in 1910. It is inherently hydrophobic and does not dissolve in water, which is the primary reason for its poor bioavailability when consumed alone.

### Bioavailability Problem and Solutions

Curcumin's low bioavailability is caused by poor gastrointestinal absorption, rapid metabolism in the liver to glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, and a short half-life of approximately 1.7 hours in rats.

The most effective and accessible solution is co-administration with piperine, the active compound in black pepper. Taking curcumin with piperine increases serum absorption by more than 2000-fold. This explains the traditional Indian cooking practice of combining turmeric with black pepper in spice blends and curries.

Other delivery systems include:

- Phospholipid complexes such as Meriva and Longvida, which improve bioavailability 5 to 29-fold compared to standard curcumin
- Solid lipid nanoparticles, liposomes, micelles, and nanoemulsions developed for pharmaceutical applications
- Consuming curcumin with fats, since it is lipophilic, which improves gastrointestinal absorption

## Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism

Inflammation is central to many chronic diseases. Curcumin's anti-inflammatory action works primarily through inhibition of the NF-kB (nuclear factor kappa B) signaling pathway. NF-kB is activated by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-alpha and, when active, triggers downstream inflammatory mediators including COX-2 (cyclooxygenase-2), LOX (lipoxygenase), and iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase).

Curcumin blocks NF-kB activation and suppresses these downstream pathways, reducing circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. It also inhibits the expression of CXCL1 and CXCL2, chemokines associated with tumor metastasis.

Clinical evidence includes: reduction of knee pain in osteoarthritis patients using 180mg/day Theracurmin for 8 weeks; improvement in rheumatoid arthritis symptoms at 500mg/day over 8 weeks; and reduction in inflammatory bowel disease symptoms at 0.35-0.55g/day over 1-2 months.

---

## Antioxidant Activity

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) including superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide cause damage to nucleic acids and DNA, contributing to tumor formation and accelerated aging. Curcumin scavenges these free radicals and also inhibits ROS-generating enzymes such as xanthine oxidase and lipoxygenase.

Turmeric ranks first among spices on the Oxygen Radical Absorption Capacity (ORAC) scale, with a value of 44,776. This is significantly higher than most other common spices.

---

## Antimicrobial Activity

Curcumin inhibits the growth of antibiotic-resistant strains including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from burn wound infections. It shows synergism with meropenem against these organisms.

Against Helicobacter pylori, the bacterium responsible for most stomach ulcers, curcumin demonstrates antibacterial activity with MIC values of 5-50 mcg/mL against 65 clinical isolates. Among all the plant extracts tested for H. pylori activity including cumin, ginger, chili, and licorice, turmeric was found to be the most efficient.

## Digestive Properties

As a digestive stimulant, turmeric enhances the activities of pancreatic lipase, chymotrypsin, and amylase. When combined with other spices such as coriander, red chili, black pepper, and cumin, it produces a pronounced stimulation of bile flow and bile acid secretion. This explains why turmeric-containing spice blends like curry powder are consistently associated with improved fat digestion.

In animal models, turmeric increased mucin content of gastric juice in rabbits. In human studies, 1.5g/day of turmeric for 30 days significantly reduced urinary excretion of mutagens in chronic smokers, and 2 capsules of 300mg taken five times daily healed peptic ulcers in 48% of patients after 4 weeks and 76% after 12 weeks.

## Other Documented Health Effects

**Cardiovascular:** Improves serum lipid levels; reduces LDL oxidation; lowers triglycerides. Clinical trials show 29% increase in HDL cholesterol and 11.63% decrease in total serum cholesterol at 0.5g/day.

**Diabetes:** Suppresses blood glucose elevation in type 2 diabetic KK-Ay mice; elevates postprandial serum insulin levels in humans at 6g of C. longa; improves insulin sensitivity at 0.6g/day over 8 weeks.

**Neuroprotective:** Curcumin crosses the blood-brain barrier. It stimulates neurogenesis via Notch and Wnt/beta-catenin pathways, reduces amyloid-beta plaque accumulation, and has demonstrated antidepressant effects comparable to fluoxetine in clinical trials.

**Exercise recovery:** 200mg curcumin before and after a 45-minute downhill run significantly reduced delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and lowered IL-8 levels 2 hours post-exercise compared to placebo.

## Safety

The FDA classifies turmeric and curcumin as GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) at doses up to 4,000-8,000 mg/day. The JECFA acceptable daily intake is 0-3 mg/kg body weight. Side effects at high doses include abdominal pain, nausea, diarrhea, and yellow stools. People on blood thinners, about to have surgery, pregnant, or undergoing chemotherapy should consult a doctor before supplementing.

Typical dietary consumption in India is 200-1000 mg of turmeric per day. Rural areas average 600 mg/day and urban areas approximately 200 mg/day.
